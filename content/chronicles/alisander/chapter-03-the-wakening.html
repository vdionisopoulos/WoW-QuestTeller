Deathknell — Tirisfal Glades

Three Ways to Wake

Alisander had assumed that surviving resurrection was the difficult part.

He was beginning to understand that he had been wrong.

The grave returned the body only once.

Everything that followed was a negotiation.

With memory.

With fear.

With whatever remained of the person who had occupied the flesh before death claimed it.

Caretaker Caice found him near the edge of the graveyard, where Alisander had been watching another newly risen corpse struggle upright beneath the diseased trees.

The caretaker possessed the weary impatience of someone who had witnessed the same tragedy too many times for sentimentality to survive.

Three of the newly awakened were having difficulty adjusting.

Caice wanted someone to speak with them.

Alisander almost laughed.

A short while ago he had been gathering embalming supplies.

Then he had been destroying corpses whose minds had failed to return.

Now apparently he had been promoted to counsellor of the dead.

Deathknell had an extraordinary talent for turning existential horror into administrative work.

Yet he accepted.

Not because Caice needed him.

Because Alisander did.

Six Mindless Zombies had shown him what happened when nothing meaningful returned with the body.

These three might show him something far more useful.

What happened when the mind did return—

and wished that it hadn't.

◆

Marshal Redpath

The first was a soldier.

Alisander knew that before the man spoke.

Even in death, discipline remained visible.

Marshal Redpath stood rigid among the graves as though Deathknell were merely an unfamiliar battlefield and someone had neglected to provide him with proper orders.

His clothing was ruined.

His flesh was dead.

Neither fact appeared particularly important to him.

What mattered was the war.

There was always a war.

Redpath spoke of enemies, positions and duties with growing intensity.

The world inside his head had not caught up with the world surrounding his corpse.

Alisander listened.

At first he thought the marshal was confused.

Then he realized the truth was more complicated.

Redpath remembered.

Perhaps too well.

His memories had become walls.

Behind them, the dead soldier could continue pretending that nothing fundamental had changed.

Alisander looked at the man's decomposing hands.

Redpath did not.

Some minds survived death by refusing to acknowledge that it had happened.

For the first time, Alisander wondered whether memory itself could become a kind of madness.

He had been desperate to recover his past.

Redpath made him reconsider the cost.

Perhaps remembering everything was not the same as understanding anything.

◆

Lilian Voss

The second was different.

Very different.

Lilian Voss stood apart from the others.

She did not look confused.

She looked horrified.

Not by Deathknell.

Not by the graves.

By herself.

Alisander recognized the expression immediately.

He had felt something similar when he first raised his own gray hand beneath the poisoned sky.

But where his horror had become curiosity, hers had hardened into rejection.

Lilian wanted no part of what she had become.

The word Forsaken meant nothing to her.

Brotherhood meant nothing.

Survival meant nothing.

She wanted the world to be wrong.

She wanted her eyes to lie.

She wanted the corpse wearing her name to belong to someone else.

Alisander said little.

There were wounds language could not close.

Lilian stared at her hands as though they belonged to an enemy.

Perhaps, to her, they did.

“What if accepting what we are is the thing that finally makes it true?”

The question remained between them.

Alisander had no answer.

He had accepted facts quickly.

No heartbeat.

No warmth.

No breath.

Therefore: dead.

Simple.

Logical.

But Lilian reminded him that identity was not arithmetic.

One could understand a truth completely and still be unable to live with it.

Or unlive with it.

The distinction seemed increasingly irrelevant.

◆

Valdred Moray

The third surprised him most.

Valdred Moray was afraid.

That was obvious.

But fear had not consumed him.

Neither had denial.

He understood that he was dead.

He understood that the life he remembered had ended.

And somehow, impossibly, he had begun considering what came next.

Alisander studied him.

There was no heroic revelation.

No sudden embrace of undeath.

Valdred simply seemed prepared to continue.

One moment at a time.

One decision at a time.

It was remarkably ordinary.

Alisander found that almost profound.

Redpath lived inside yesterday.

Lilian fought against today.

Valdred, somehow, had begun thinking about tomorrow.

Perhaps acceptance was not surrender.
Perhaps it was merely choosing where to fight.

That thought followed Alisander long after their conversation ended.

◆

Three Corpses

Alisander returned slowly.

Three newly awakened Forsaken.

Three functioning minds.

Three entirely different reactions.

Redpath had preserved himself through denial.

Lilian had preserved herself through rejection.

Valdred had begun preserving himself through adaptation.

None appeared mindless.

Yet none appeared whole.

The realization bothered Alisander.

His question had been wrong.

He had divided the resurrected into two categories.

Those who returned with their minds.

And those who did not.

Reality was considerably less convenient.

A mind could survive and still fracture.

Memory could survive and become a prison.

Identity could survive and become unbearable.

Reason could survive while everything that gave reason meaning collapsed around it.

There were apparently a thousand ways to return incorrectly.

The six Mindless had been the simplest case.

That thought disturbed him deeply.

The Fourth Answer

Caretaker Caice asked whether the three would manage.

Alisander looked back toward the graveyard.

Redpath still stood like a soldier waiting for orders from a world that no longer existed.

Lilian had withdrawn into herself, fighting a war no spell could touch.

Valdred remained frightened but upright.

“I don't know,” Alisander said.

The words irritated him.

He disliked not knowing.

Caice merely shrugged.

Perhaps the caretaker had learned long ago that resurrection did not come with guarantees.

Alisander had not.

He intended to.

He walked away from the resurrection grounds, turning the encounters over in his mind.

Three answers to undeath.

Deny it.

Reject it.

Endure it.

Alisander disliked all three.

Denial surrendered reason.

Rejection surrendered the future.

Endurance surrendered too much to chance.

There had to be another answer.

He stopped.

Blue-white light gathered around his fingertips.

The Arcane responded immediately.

Structure.

Knowledge.

Will.

Those principles had survived the grave.

Perhaps they could guide everything else.

If death had changed the rules of his existence,
then Alisander would learn the new rules.

He would not pretend to be the man who had died.

He would not despise the corpse that remained.

And he would not simply wait to discover what undeath intended to make of him.

He would choose.

Not once.

Every day.

Every spell.

Every decision.

Whatever Alisander had been before death was history.

Whatever he became afterward—

that would be his responsibility.

◆

The Road Out of Deathknell

Something had changed.

Not in his body.

That remained stubbornly dead.

The change was smaller.

More dangerous.

Alisander was beginning to develop an opinion about what he was.

He was Forsaken.

He was a mage.

He was dead.

All three statements were true.

None was sufficient.

Somewhere beyond Deathknell waited the ruins of Lordaeron, the Undercity and a world populated largely by people who would consider his continued existence an abomination.

There would be time to deal with them.

First he needed to understand the creatures standing beside him.

His people.

The phrase felt strange.

He allowed it to remain.

Ahead, another voice called from among the dead.

Another task.

Another problem.

Alisander began walking.

Deathknell was supposed to be where the dead were buried.

Instead, it was teaching him how to live.

Poorly, perhaps.

But one should not demand miracles.

He had already received one.

The question was no longer why Alisander had returned.
The question was what he intended to do with the inconvenience.
