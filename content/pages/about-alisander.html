Background

What Alisander Remembers

Alisander's known life begins twice: once somewhere beyond the reach of his surviving memory, and once beneath the dead trees of Deathknell.

He remembers fragments of the man who existed before undeath. Books. Candles. Ink staining his fingers. The exacting architecture of spellcraft and the certainty that a single misplaced syllable could transform controlled magic into catastrophe.

He remembers Arcane power as something beautiful, precise and hungry. He does not remember his final moments clearly. Somewhere behind the darkness are screams, pain, perhaps streets filled with the dead—and then nothing.

What returned in Deathknell was therefore not a complete biography. It was a mind, a discipline, a dry sense of humor, and a refusal to accept that mystery was the same thing as meaninglessness.

His first certainty after death was not that he remained the same man. It was that the mage remained. Magic answered his hand. From that point onward, Alisander's story became a search for everything else: why he returned, what separates a Forsaken mind from the Mindless, what parts of identity survive death, and what he will choose to become now that his old life can no longer decide for him.

Memory Fragments

  • Books and long study.
  • Candlelight and ink-stained fingers.
  • Precise knowledge of Arcane spellcraft.
  • Screaming somewhere near the end of his former life.
  • Pain followed by absolute darkness.
  • No reliable memory yet of the person he was before death.
Personality

The Man Who Came Back

Alisander approaches undeath like a problem that ought to have rules. He is analytical, skeptical and more comfortable with the precision of magic than the uncertainty of emotion. His humor is dry enough to survive his own funeral, and his instinct when confronted with an impossible question is not to abandon it but to dissect it.

Beneath that control is a growing fear: not of dying again, but of losing the mind that survived the first death. The Mindless have already shown him that a corpse can continue without the person inside it. That possibility gives his search for knowledge a deeply personal urgency.
